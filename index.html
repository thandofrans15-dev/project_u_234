The control room is where a licensed crew keeps the reactor inside its safe operating limits, hour by hour. Nothing here is automatic in the sense of "unsupervised" — automatic systems act fast, but a human crew watches every one of them and can act faster than any single one on its own.

NEUTRON FLUX / POWER RECORDER
A continuous trace of reactor power, usually 0–120% of rated output. This is the single number the whole shift watches most closely — it's the direct readout of the fission rate itself.
ROD POSITION INDICATORS
A readout — often a vertical bar per rod or rod bank — showing exactly how far each control rod is inserted, in steps or percent. Operators use this to confirm the core's actual reactivity margin, not just the commanded one.
COOLANT TEMPERATURE & PRESSURE GAUGES
Inlet and outlet coolant temperature, plus system pressure. The gap between inlet and outlet temperature tells the crew how much heat the core is actually producing.
MANUAL ROD CONTROL SWITCHES
A key-locked switch or rotary control that lets an operator withdraw or insert a rod bank in small, deliberate steps — normally used only for small adjustments, since automatic control handles routine regulation.
ALARM ANNUNCIATOR PANEL
A wall of labeled lights that stay dark in normal operation and light up (often with a horn) the instant any parameter — temperature, pressure, flow, radiation — leaves its allowed band. Crews are trained to silence the horn, then diagnose, in that order.
RADIATION MONITORS
Continuous readouts of radiation levels in the containment, the coolant, and the plant's exhaust stack — an early, independent check that fuel cladding is doing its job.
CONTAINMENT STATUS PANEL
Shows whether containment isolation valves are open or sealed, and whether containment pressure is within its normal band — the crew's confirmation that the last safety barrier is intact.
SHIFT LOG & PROCEDURES
Every action — a rod move, an alarm, a test — is logged by hand or by the plant computer and checked against a written procedure. Nothing safety-relevant happens "from memory" alone.
THE SCRAM / EMERGENCY SHUTDOWN BUTTON
One clearly guarded button (called AZ-5 at Chernobyl, "SCRAM" in most Western plants). Pressing it releases every control rod at once — usually by cutting power to electromagnets holding them up — so they fall or spring into the core within seconds, absorbing enough neutrons to stop the chain reaction almost immediately. It fires automatically on serious trip conditions (high power, low coolant flow, high pressure, loss of power) and can always be triggered manually by the crew.

In modern SMR control rooms, much of this is digitized onto screens rather than physical dials and switches — but the same categories of information and the same emergency button are always present, by regulation.

03 Nuclear World
A global safety study

SMRs are already running, under construction, or in active planning on nearly every continent. The engineering changes site to site — the safety framework underneath doesn't.

◎

One Shared Rulebook

The IAEA sets baseline safety standards most national regulators build on, so a plant is judged against a broadly similar bar wherever it's built.

⛨

Defense-in-Depth, Everywhere

Fuel cladding, vessel, cooling backups, containment — the same layered "if this fails, the next layer catches it" logic, on a barge or a coastline.

✦

Engineered for the Site

Floating plants shrug off pack ice; desert designs lean on air cooling. Small factory-built reactors tune to local climate far more easily than giant plants.

⬡

Fuel That's Hard to Divert

Most SMR fuel is low-enriched, often sealed in factory-loaded cartridges never opened on site, with IAEA safeguards tracking it start to finish.
